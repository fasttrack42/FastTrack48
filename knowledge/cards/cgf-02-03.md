# CGF ch2: A Brief History of Fasting · ch3: Busting the Myths of Fasting

### CGF-02-001
claim: Nearly every religious tradition sets regular fast days. Weekly, lunar and seasonal fasts form a built-in intermittent-fasting calendar.
why: Hindu lunar and weekday fasts, Orthodox Christian fasts on up to 180–200 days a year, Buddhist monks eating nothing after noon, Ramadan, and Monday/Thursday fasts in Islam.
grade: D
src: CGF ch2
safety: none
when: fastday | history
aud: all
tier: core
note: Anchors the app's lunar/Ekadashi feature in the wider tradition.

### CGF-02-002
claim: Crete, the original model for the "Mediterranean diet", was also a population that fasted on many days of the year under Orthodox tradition.
why: Ancel Keys's studies of Crete focused on food choices; the frequent fasting practised there was largely left out of the story.
grade: D
src: CGF ch2
counter: Whether fasting contributed to Cretan longevity is speculation.
safety: none
when: history | fastday
aud: all
tier: tail

claim: Ramadan is the most studied religious fast, but unlike health fasts it forbids fluids, causing daily mild dehydration.
why: Sunrise-to-sunset dry fasting combines food restriction with fluid restriction, so its research results don't map directly onto water fasts.

### CGF-02-005
claim: Losing appetite when ill is a shared instinct across mammals; the body deliberately turns hunger down during infection.
why: Sickness-induced anorexia frees energy and shifts metabolism toward defense. Fasting is one of the body's own responses, not an artificial imposition.
grade: C
src: CGF ch2; Wang 2016 Cell
counter: In mice, fasting helped survival in bacterial infection but harmed it in viral infection. Don't force fasting during illness.
safety: none
when: history | myth
aud: all
tier: tail

### CGF-02-006
claim: Medicine used supervised fasting for obesity in the 1950s–60s, including absolute fasts of 14 days or more.
why: Bloom (1959), Gilliland (1968) and Drenick (1964) reported steady weight loss in hospital. Interest faded with the turn toward dietary-fat research.
grade: B
src: CGF ch2; Gilliland 1968 Postgrad Med J; Drenick 1964 JAMA; Folin & Denis 1915 J Biol Chem
counter: Fung leaves out that some prolonged therapeutic fasts ended in serious complications and a few deaths (see SCI-02-001).
safety: none
when: history
aud: all
tier: tail

### SCI-02-001
claim: Very long water-only fasts carried real risks in the 1960s hospital programs: rare cardiac deaths and serious complications.
why: Reports included heart-muscle damage, dangerous arrhythmias, lactic acidosis and severe refeeding problems, mostly in fasts of many weeks without monitoring.
do: Never fast beyond about 3 days alone. Longer fasts need medical supervision and blood tests.
grade: B
src: Garnett 1969 Lancet (PMID 4180896); Spencer 1968 Lancet (PMID 4175509); Runcie & Thomson 1970 Br Med J (PMID 5454322)
safety: stop
when: long | plan | stage:72+
aud: all
tier: core
note: VERIFIED all three exist (titles: cardiac myofibril fragmentation; death during therapeutic starvation; 'a dangerous procedure?').

### CGF-03-001
claim: Months of daily calorie cutting lower your metabolism substantially: the "starvation mode" people fear from fasting.
why: In long calorie-restricted studies metabolic rate fell 25–40%, partly from lost mass and partly from adaptation. Cold, fatigue and hunger follow, so weight returns easily.
grade: A
src: CGF ch3; Keys 1950 Biology of Human Starvation; Johannsen 2012 J Clin Endocrinol Metab; YT tIuj-oMN-Fk
counter: Some of the drop simply reflects a smaller body. The adaptive part beyond that is real but smaller, around 5–15%.
safety: none
when: myth | plateau | onboarding
aud: all
tier: core

### CGF-03-002
claim: Three weeks of alternate-day fasting did not lower resting metabolism. Fat burning rose ~58% while sugar burning fell ~53%.
why: The body switched fuel, not throttle: stored fat replaced dietary carbohydrate, and total energy use held steady.
grade: B
src: CGF ch3; Heilbronn 2005 Am J Clin Nutr
counter: 22 days, 16 non-obese adults. Longer RCTs find alternate-day fasting about equal to daily calorie restriction for weight (Trepanowski 2017 JAMA Intern Med).
safety: none
when: plan | myth
aud: all
tier: tail

### CGF-03-003
claim: In a 10-week alternate-day fasting study, about 6% weight loss came almost entirely from fat; lean mass didn't change.
why: Fat mass fell ~11% while fat-free mass held. Intermittent fasting appeared to spare lean tissue in this trial.
grade: B
src: CGF ch3; Bhutani 2010 Obesity
counter: Small trial. Other studies find lean loss similar to calorie restriction (~25% of weight lost), and higher in some time-restricted eating trials (Lowe 2020 JAMA Intern Med).
safety: none
when: myth | plan
aud: all
tier: tail

### CGF-03-004
claim: Fung says the body only turns to muscle when body fat is nearly gone (under ~4%).
why: His reasoning: fat is the energy store and muscle is functional tissue, so evolution should protect muscle until fat runs out.
grade: D
src: CGF ch3
counter: Not supported as stated. Protein is broken down from the first day at a falling rate; it is spared, not untouched (see CGF-01-008).
safety: none
when: myth
aud: advanced
tier: drop
note: Do not ship. Kept so the claim's correction is on record.

### CGF-03-005
claim: Resistance exercise and enough protein while eating are what protect muscle; fasting doesn't replace either.
why: Muscle responds to load. Combining fasting or time-restricted eating with strength training preserves lean mass far better than fasting alone.
do: Lift or do bodyweight training 2–3×/week, and eat ample protein (~1.2–1.6 g/kg) within your eating window.
grade: A
src: CGF ch3; Morton 2018 Br J Sports Med; Moro 2016 J Transl Med
counter: Fung says supplements like whey and creatine don't build muscle; meta-analyses show modest added gains when combined with training.
safety: none
when: eat | between | myth
aud: all
tier: core

### CGF-03-007
claim: In healthy people fasting doesn't cause dangerous low blood sugar; the liver keeps glucose in range, overnight and for days.
why: Glycogen covers the first day; then the liver makes new glucose from glycerol and amino acids while ketones feed the brain.
do: If you take insulin, sulfonylureas or other glucose-lowering drugs, fasting can cause severe lows: get a medication plan from your doctor first.
grade: A
src: CGF ch3; Cahill 2006 Annu Rev Nutr
safety: caution
when: onboarding | myth | start
aud: all, t2d, meds
tier: core

### CGF-03-008
claim: The brain is not glucose-only. Once fat-burning is under way it runs largely on ketones, so long fasts don't starve your thinking.
why: Ketones cross into the brain and replace much of its glucose need, the human brain's backup fuel for food scarcity.
grade: A
src: CGF ch3; Owen 1967 J Clin Invest
safety: none
when: stage:20-24 | myth
aud: all
tier: core

### CGF-03-009
claim: Many fasters report clear-headedness and calm in fasts. Ketones and a steadier fuel supply are the likely reason.
why: Ketone metabolism and the absence of blood-sugar swings may stabilize energy and mood; noradrenaline adds alertness.
grade: C
src: CGF ch3
counter: Mostly self-report. Small studies note mood improvement in fasting (Fond 2013 Psychiatry Res), but placebo and expectation effects are large.
safety: none

aud: all

### CGF-03-010
claim: After a 36-hour fast, people eat only ~20% more the next day, leaving a net deficit of ~2,000 kcal over the two days.
why: Compensation is partial. Hunger after a fast doesn't come close to replacing the skipped meals.
do: Don't fear a rebound: eat normally after a fast, without "making up" for it.
grade: B
src: CGF ch3; Johnstone 2002 Int J Obes
safety: none
when: break | myth
aud: all
tier: core

### CGF-03-012
claim: There is no essential dietary carbohydrate. The body can make all the glucose it needs; essential amino acids and fats must come from food.
why: The liver synthesizes glucose; it can't make essential amino acids or omega-3/6 fats, so those need to be eaten between fasts.
do: Make your eating windows protein- and nutrient-rich; for fasts beyond a few days, take a multivitamin.
grade: A
src: CGF ch3
safety: none
when: eat | long
aud: all
tier: tail

### CGF-03-013
claim: Even a lean adult carries enough body fat to cover over a month of energy needs.
why: A 70 kg person at 15% body fat stores about 10 kg of fat, ~80,000 kcal, versus ~2,000 kcal of glycogen.
grade: A
src: CGF ch3; CGF intro
safety: none
