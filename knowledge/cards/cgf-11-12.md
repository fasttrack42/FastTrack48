# CGF ch11: Kinds of Fasts and Best Practices · ch12: Intermittent Fasting

### CGF-11-001
claim: Allowed while fasting: still or sparkling water, any tea including herbal, and natural flavors like lemon, mint or cinnamon.
why: These carry almost no energy, so insulin stays low, while they ease hunger, keep you hydrated and preserve daily rituals.
grade: D
src: CGF ch11
safety: none
when: fluids | start
aud: all
tier: core
note: Phase-2 candidate: a quick "Can I have…?" lookup from the fasting screen.

### CGF-11-002
claim: Not allowed: sugar, honey, agave, juice, artificial sweeteners and flavors (diet drinks, flavored water mixes).
why: Sugars break the fast outright. Fung excludes sweeteners and artificial flavors because they can stoke hunger and run against the purpose of the fast.
grade: D
src: CGF ch11
safety: none
when: fluids
aud: all
tier: core

### CGF-11-005
claim: Aim for about 2 liters of fluid a day while fasting, and start each morning with a glass of water.
why: Part of normal fluid intake comes from food, so fasting removes it; losing salt and water when insulin falls adds to dehydration risk.
grade: D
src: CGF ch11
safety: none
when: fluids | start
aud: all
tier: core

### CGF-11-007
claim: Green tea may help suppress appetite during a fast; cinnamon and ginger teas are traditional hunger-quieters, and mint or chamomile calm.
why: Green-tea catechins are thought to influence appetite and fat metabolism; warm drinks also fill the stomach and satisfy the ritual.
grade: C
src: CGF ch11
counter: Human appetite data for catechins are weak and inconsistent; the calming, ritual effect is the reliable part.
safety: none
when: fluids | hunger
aud: all
tier: tail

### CGF-11-008
claim: A little apple-cider vinegar in water may blunt the blood-sugar rise from the next meal.
why: Acetic acid appears to slow stomach emptying and improve muscle glucose uptake; small trials show lower post-meal glucose.
do: Always dilute it (1–2 tablespoons in a large glass) to protect teeth and throat.
grade: B
src: CGF ch11; Johnston 2004 Diabetes Care
counter: Small, short trials; the effect is modest.
safety: none
when: fluids | break
aud: all
tier: tail

### CGF-11-009
claim: A strict water-only fast includes no salt, so it carries more risk of dehydration, especially beyond a couple of days.
why: Without salt the body holds less water; kidneys conserve sodium well, but long water-only fasts still risk dizziness and low blood pressure.
do: For fasts longer than ~2 days, add salt to your water unless your doctor has told you to restrict it.
grade: B
src: CGF ch11
safety: caution
when: long | fluids | symptom:dizzy
aud: all
tier: core

### CGF-11-010
claim: Fruit-juice "fasts" aren't fasts: the sugar keeps insulin up. Green vegetable juices carry far less sugar.
why: Fruit juices concentrate fructose and glucose without fiber, so they behave like sugary drinks.
grade: B
src: CGF ch11
safety: none
when: fluids | plan | myth
aud: all
tier: tail

### CGF-11-011
claim: A "fat fast" (only cream, butter, coconut oil or a few nuts during the fast) is a soft option some people use to get through.
why: Pure fat barely raises insulin, and a small bite can rescue a fast that would otherwise be abandoned.
grade: D
src: CGF ch11 (Amy Berger)
counter: Scientific evidence is scarce. It adds calories that delay drawing on body fat.
safety: none
when: hunger | fluids
aud: advanced
tier: tail

### CGF-11-012
claim: The fasting-mimicking diet (5 days of ~700–1,100 kcal a month) aims to recreate fasting's effects while still eating.
why: Low protein and calorie intake lower IGF-1 and glucose much as fasting does, but with food provided.
grade: B
src: CGF ch11; Wei 2017 Sci Transl Med
counter: Fung considers it needlessly complex. It does have RCT data (3 monthly cycles improved weight and risk markers).
safety: none
when: plan | long
aud: advanced
tier: tail

### CGF-12-001
claim: There's no single best fasting schedule. The right one is the one you can repeat, and you can switch anytime.
why: Shorter daily fasts suit weight control and busy lives; longer, less frequent fasts act faster on blood sugar and fatty liver.
grade: D
src: CGF ch12
safety: none
when: plan | onboarding
aud: all
tier: core

### CGF-12-002
claim: The first few fasts are always the hardest. Fasting gets measurably easier with repetition.
why: Hunger habits, cephalic responses and fuel switching all adapt; your body learns to move into fat-burning faster.
do: Give it a month. Judge fasting by your fifth attempt, not your first.
grade: D
src: CGF ch12; CGF ch15
safety: none
when: start | break | between
aud: beginner
tier: core

### CGF-12-003
claim: A 12-hour overnight fast (e.g. 7 p.m. to 7 a.m.) was normal until the 1970s. It's a good baseline for prevention.
why: It gives one daily low-insulin window. Fung considers it protective but usually not enough on its own to reverse weight gain.
grade: D
src: CGF ch12
safety: none
when: plan
aud: beginner
tier: core

### CGF-12-004
claim: 16:8 (an 8-hour eating window, usually skipping breakfast) is the easiest daily fast to sustain, since sleep covers half of it.
why: Most people aren't hungry in the morning, so moving the first meal to midday creates a 16-hour fast with little effort.
do: Pair it with less sugar and refined carbs; Fung says the window alone works slowly.
grade: B
src: CGF ch12
counter: In RCTs, an 8-hour window alone gave little weight loss beyond normal eating (Lowe 2020). With equal calories it matched daily calorie restriction (Liu 2022 N Engl J Med).
safety: none
when: plan
aud: all
tier: core

### SCI-12-001
claim: Time-restricted eating alone usually gives modest weight loss; benefits come mostly from eating less and better within the window.
why: Large RCTs (TREAT 2020, Liu 2022) found 16:8 or similar no better than equal-calorie eating without a window for weight.
do: Use the window as a structure, then fill it with whole, protein-rich food and no grazing.
grade: A
src: Lowe 2020 JAMA Intern Med; Liu 2022 N Engl J Med
counter: Earlier eating windows (early TRE) improved insulin sensitivity and blood pressure even without weight loss (Sutton 2018 Cell Metab).
safety: none
when: plan | plateau
aud: all
tier: core
note: Counterweight to Fung's TRE optimism; keeps in-app claims honest.

### CGF-12-005
claim: An appetite reset from fasting only lasts while the schedule is kept, but a day or two off doesn't erase it.
why: Hunger patterns adapt to routine over weeks; brief breaks are absorbed, abandoning the schedule gradually undoes it.
do: Missed a fast or had a feast day? Just resume tomorrow. No need to start over.
grade: D
src: CGF ch12 (Bert Herring)
safety: none
when: between | break
aud: all
tier: core

### CGF-12-006
claim: The same meal raises blood sugar and insulin more in the evening than in the morning.
why: Insulin sensitivity follows the body clock and is worst at night; one classic study found 25–50% higher insulin response to evening meals.
do: Make dinner lighter and earlier when you can; avoid large late-night meals.
grade: A
src: CGF ch12; Van Cauter 1992 Am J Physiol
safety: none
when: eat | plan
aud: all
tier: core

### CGF-12-007
claim: Eating your largest meal earlier in the day (breakfast or lunch) and a light dinner may help weight and blood sugar.
why: Morning insulin sensitivity is higher. In one trial, a big-breakfast group lost more weight than a big-dinner group on equal calories.
grade: B
src: CGF ch12; Jakubowicz 2013 Obesity; Sutton 2018 Cell Metab
counter: A tightly controlled 2022 trial found no weight difference between big breakfast and big dinner, only less hunger with the big breakfast (Ruddick-Collins 2022 Cell Metab).
safety: none
when: eat | plan
aud: all
tier: core

### CGF-12-008
claim: Night-shift work is linked to higher rates of obesity and diabetes, partly because meals fall when the body handles food worst.
why: Eating at biological night meets low insulin sensitivity; disrupted sleep raises cortisol and appetite.
do: On night shifts, keep the main meal before the shift and avoid heavy eating overnight.
grade: B
src: CGF ch12
safety: none
when: eat | plan
aud: all
tier: tail

### CGF-12-009
claim: Hard-training athletes and highly stressed people should introduce fasting gradually. Chronic under-eating backfires.
why: Fasting adds a stressor; combined with heavy training or life stress it can push into energy deficiency, hurting performance, sleep and hormones.
grade: D
src: CGF ch12 (Robb Wolf)
safety: none
when: plan
aud: advanced
tier: tail

### CGF-12-010
claim: The 20:4 "Warrior Diet" eats all food in a 4-hour evening window, a 20-hour daily fast.
why: A longer daily fast deepens the low-insulin period; Hofmekler pairs it with whole foods and interval training.
grade: D
src: CGF ch12
counter: The evening window conflicts with the circadian evidence favoring earlier eating (CGF-12-006).
