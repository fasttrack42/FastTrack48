# CGF ch9: What You Need to Know About Hunger · ch10: Who Should Not Fast?

### CGF-09-001
claim: Hunger comes in waves, not a steady climb. A wave builds, peaks and fades, often within an hour, even if you eat nothing.
why: Ghrelin, the hunger hormone, spikes around your usual mealtimes and then drops again on its own, whether or not food arrives.
do: When a wave hits, drink something warm (tea or hot water), wait 20–30 minutes, and move on to a task. It passes.
grade: B
src: CGF ch9; Natalucci 2005 Eur J Endocrinol
safety: none
when: hunger | stage:12-20 | stage:20-24
aud: all
tier: core

### CGF-09-002
claim: Morning is usually the least hungry time of day, even after 12–14 hours without food. Evening is the hungriest.
why: Appetite follows the body clock: the circadian drive for hunger is lowest around 8 a.m. and highest around 8 p.m., regardless of when you last ate.
do: Extending the overnight fast by skipping breakfast is often the easiest first step.
grade: B
src: CGF ch9; Scheer 2013 Obesity
safety: none
when: plan | hunger | start
aud: beginner
tier: core

### CGF-09-003
claim: Much of what we call hunger is learned. Clocks, places, screens and smells can trigger it like Pavlov's bell.
why: Repeated pairing of food with times and situations conditions the brain to release saliva, insulin and hunger signals on cue: the cephalic phase response.
do: Notice the trigger ("it's noon", "I'm on the couch") and label it: cue, not need.
grade: A
src: CGF ch9
safety: none
when: hunger | between
aud: all
tier: core

### CGF-09-004
claim: Eating only at a table, never at the desk, in the car, in bed or in front of a screen, unwires many hunger cues.
why: When food is tied to one place, other places stop triggering it. This was ordinary habit for earlier generations.

### CGF-09-005
claim: Swap a habit rather than just dropping it. Replace the evening snack with a cup of herbal or green tea.
why: Stopping a routine leaves a gap that pulls you back; substituting a harmless ritual keeps the cue but changes the response, as gum does for ex-smokers.
do: Pick one replacement ritual for your usual snack time and keep it for two weeks.
grade: B

### CGF-09-006
claim: Diet drinks and sweeteners can make fasting harder; replacing diet drinks with water led to more weight loss in a 24-week trial.
why: Sweet taste may trigger the cephalic hunger and insulin response without supplying energy, leaving appetite primed but unsatisfied.
do: Fast without sweeteners if you can. If you need some, use a little, and drop it if hunger worsens or progress stalls.
grade: B
src: CGF ch9; Madjd 2015 Am J Clin Nutr
counter: Whether sweeteners themselves trigger insulin in humans is inconsistent across studies; effects vary by person and sweetener.
safety: none
when: fluids | hunger
aud: all
tier: core

### CGF-09-007
claim: Smelling, seeing or cooking food while fasting is the hardest test there is. Avoid it rather than resist it.
why: Food cues fully trigger the cephalic hunger response. Failing to resist it is physiology, not weak willpower.
do: Don't cook, browse food or shop on fasting days; leave the kitchen during family meals if you need to.

### CGF-09-009
claim: Write an if-then list for hunger moments, e.g. "If I open the fridge, I walk for ten minutes instead."
why: Pre-planned responses to a specific cue ("implementation intentions") reliably beat willpower in behavior research.
do: Keep 3–5 quick actions ready: water, a walk, a call, a small chore, stepping outside.

### CGF-09-010
claim: In longer fasts hunger typically peaks around day 2 and then fades. Many people feel unusually well afterward.
why: Hunger is hormonal, not a gauge of an empty stomach. As ketones rise, appetite signals quiet down; hospital fasting reports note little hunger after the first days.
grade: B
src: CGF ch9; CGF ch12; Gilliland 1968 Postgrad Med J; Espelund 2005 J Clin Endocrinol Metab
safety: none

aud: all

### CGF-09-011
claim: Fung sometimes advises people stuck at 24 hours to try a 3–7 day fast: once day 2 passes, they learn first-hand that hunger fades.
why: A longer fast lets the body fully switch to fat and ketones, which resets expectations about hunger faster than repeated short attempts.
grade: D
src: CGF ch9

### CGF-10-001
claim: Don't fast if you're underweight or malnourished. Fung advises against any fasting below a BMI of about 20.
why: Without enough fat to draw on, the body breaks down functional tissue; complication risk climbs steeply at low body weight.
do: Check your BMI (weight in kg ÷ height in m²). Under 20, don't use fasting for health.
grade: A
src: CGF ch10
safety: stop
when: onboarding | plan
aud: all
tier: core

### CGF-10-002
claim: If you have or have had an eating disorder, don't fast. It can feed restriction or binge cycles.
why: Deliberate restriction overlaps with the core behaviors of anorexia and bulimia, and can trigger relapse in people with that history.
do: If food or weight feels out of control, talk to a professional before any fasting.
grade: B
src: CGF ch10; Hoddy 2015 Nutr J
counter: Fung argues fasting doesn't cause anorexia, and in obese adults alternate-day fasting didn't raise disordered-eating scores. Risk is in those already vulnerable.
safety: stop
when: onboarding
aud: all
tier: core

### CGF-10-003
claim: Fasting shouldn't be punishment for overeating. Use it as a planned rhythm, not penance for "cheating".
why: Compensatory restriction after eating is a recognized risk pattern for disordered eating and erodes your relationship with food.
grade: D
src: CGF ch10 (Amy Berger)
safety: caution

### CGF-10-004
claim: Uric acid rises during fasting, which can trigger gout in susceptible people. Talk to your doctor first if you've had gout.
why: Ketones compete with uric acid for excretion by the kidneys, so blood levels climb. In one study of 42 fasters, all rose but none developed gout.
do: With a gout history, stay well hydrated and ask whether to keep your uric-acid medication during fasts.
grade: B
src: CGF ch10; Runcie & Thomson 1969 Postgrad Med J
safety: caution
when: onboarding | long
aud: meds, all
tier: core

### CGF-10-005
claim: Some medicines are harsh on an empty stomach: aspirin, metformin, iron and magnesium supplements are the usual culprits.
why: Aspirin irritates the stomach lining, metformin causes nausea or diarrhea, iron causes pain and constipation, magnesium causes diarrhea; food buffers all of them.
do: Ask your doctor or pharmacist whether to take these in your eating window. Never just skip prescribed medication.
grade: A
src: CGF ch10
safety: caution

### CGF-10-006
claim: Know the signs of low blood sugar: shaking, sweating, nervousness, faintness, sudden hunger, nausea, then confusion or seizures.
why: On diabetes drugs, sugar can fall fast once food is removed. Untreated lows can be fatal.
do: If these appear, take sugar immediately (juice, glucose tablets), even if it ends the fast, then call your doctor.

### CGF-10-007
claim: Heartburn can get worse while fasting because there's no food to absorb stomach acid, though for some people it improves.
why: Abdominal fat presses on the stomach; an empty stomach still secretes acid. Weight loss over time usually relieves reflux.
do: Stop eating 3 h before bed, walk after dinner, raise the head of the bed. Ask about antacids if needed.

### CGF-10-008
claim: Women benefit from fasting as much as men; a 3-day fast left reproductive hormones and the menstrual cycle unchanged in normal-weight women.
why: Hormone disruption and lost periods come from low body fat and energy deficiency, not fasting in itself in women with adequate reserves.
grade: B
src: CGF ch10; Soules 1994 Clin Endocrinol; Drenick 1968 Am J Public Health
counter: Small studies. Lean, very active women are at higher risk of energy deficiency (RED-S); women-specific long-term data are limited.
